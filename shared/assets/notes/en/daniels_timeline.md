# Daniel's Timeline

Daniel begins with Jerusalem under judgment and ends with the faithful raised from the dead. Between those points, kingdoms rise, boast, persecute, and fall. The same court remains seated over them all. God gives the kingdom to the Son of Man and to His saints (Daniel 7:9-14, 18, 26-27).

The book contains several visions, not one continuous chart. Each vision supplies part of the picture. Daniel 2 moves from Babylon to God's indestructible kingdom. Daniel 7 repeats that movement through four beasts and the heavenly court. Daniel 8 names Medo-Persia and Greece. Daniel 9 measures seventy sevens for Jerusalem and Messiah. Daniel 10-12 follows conflict through final distress, resurrection, and Daniel's inheritance.

This study keeps three levels distinct:

- **The text:** what Daniel states or an angel explains.
- **A scriptural connection:** how another biblical passage identifies or develops Daniel's language.
- **A chronology:** a proposed historical date or calculation. A calculation may illuminate the text, but it cannot add a date that Scripture never states.

## One Story Told Through Several Visions

Daniel's visions overlap. The repeated structure helps interpret the difficult parts.

| Vision | Earthly sequence | Heavenly conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Daniel 2 | Four kingdoms, then divided strength | God's kingdom crushes every rival and fills the earth (Daniel 2:31-45) |
| Daniel 7 | Four beasts, horns, and a persecuting ruler | The court judges; the Son of Man and the saints receive the kingdom (Daniel 7:1-27) |
| Daniel 8 | Medo-Persia, Greece, four successors, and a desecrating horn | The horn is broken without human hand; the sanctuary is restored (Daniel 8:3-14, 20-25) |
| Daniel 9 | Seventy sevens for Jerusalem, Messiah, covenant, and desolation | Sin is answered and everlasting righteousness is brought in (Daniel 9:20-27) |
| Daniel 10-12 | Kings wage war; a contemptible ruler desecrates; final distress follows | God's people are delivered, the dead rise, and Daniel receives his allotted inheritance (Daniel 11:21-45; Daniel 12:1-3, 13) |

The visions do not glorify empire. Daniel calls the same political order a glittering statue in one chapter and predatory animals in another. Human rulers see precious metal. Heaven sees beasts. The kingdom of God arrives from outside that system, like a stone cut out "without human hands" (Daniel 2:34-35, 44-45).

## The Statue and the Stone: Daniel 2

Nebuchadnezzar sees a statue with a gold head, silver chest and arms, bronze belly and thighs, iron legs, and feet mixed with iron and clay. A stone strikes the feet. The whole statue collapses, and the stone becomes a mountain filling the earth (Daniel 2:31-35).

Daniel identifies Nebuchadnezzar and his kingdom as the head of gold. Three kingdoms follow. The fourth crushes like iron, then appears divided, partly strong and partly brittle (Daniel 2:37-43). During the days of those kings, God establishes a kingdom that will never be destroyed or transferred to another people (Daniel 2:44-45).

### The common kingdom sequence

| Image | Common identification | What Scripture establishes |
|---|---|---|
| Gold head | Babylon | Daniel directly names Nebuchadnezzar as the head (Daniel 2:37-38) |
| Silver chest and arms | Medo-Persia | Babylon falls to the Medes and Persians, and Daniel 8 later joins Media and Persia in one ram (Daniel 5:28-31; Daniel 8:20) |
| Bronze belly and thighs | Greece | Daniel 8 directly names Greece as the power that follows the ram (Daniel 8:5-8, 20-21) |
| Iron legs | Rome | Daniel 2 calls it the crushing fourth kingdom but does not name Rome (Daniel 2:40) |
| Iron-and-clay feet | A divided continuation of the fourth kingdom | The text stresses mixture without lasting unity (Daniel 2:41-43) |
| Stone and mountain | God's everlasting kingdom | God establishes it; it destroys the kingdoms and endures forever (Daniel 2:44-45) |

The first three names rest on Daniel's own identifications when the visions are read together. Calling the fourth kingdom Rome is the dominant Christian historical reading, but Daniel 2 never uses that name.

Another reading separates Media and Persia into the second and third kingdoms and makes Greece the fourth. It points to Darius the Mede receiving Babylon's kingdom before the reign of Cyrus the Persian (Daniel 5:28-31; Daniel 6:28). The combined Medo-Persian reading answers that Daniel 8 portrays Media and Persia as two unequal horns on one ram and then places Greece after that combined power (Daniel 8:3-7, 20-21). Daniel explicitly identifies Babylon in chapter 2 and Medo-Persia and Greece in chapter 8; the precise mapping across every image remains interpretive. A strong conclusion should be as strong as its text.

### The stone's present and future victory

Jesus identifies Himself with the rejected stone that becomes the cornerstone. He also warns that the stone will crush the one on whom it falls (Matthew 21:42-44; compare Psalm 118:22-23). His kingdom was already present in His ministry (Matthew 12:28; Luke 17:20-21), and He received authority after His resurrection (Matthew 28:18).

Yet the New Testament also looks forward to the open defeat of every hostile power. Christ reigns until every enemy, including death, is placed under His feet (1 Corinthians 15:24-28). Revelation sees the kingdoms of the world becoming the kingdom of God and His Christ (Revelation 11:15).

Some readers therefore place the stone's entire strike at Christ's future return, since the statue falls in one complete collapse (Daniel 2:34-35). Others see an inaugurated and consummated victory: God's kingdom entered history through Christ, grows by God's power, and will openly displace every rival at His return (Matthew 13:31-33). Both readings must preserve Daniel's conclusion. No human kingdom evolves into God's kingdom; God establishes it and brings the rival order to an end (Daniel 2:44-45).

## The Beasts, the Court, and the Son of Man: Daniel 7

Daniel 7 retells the kingdom sequence from heaven's point of view. Four beasts rise from a storm-tossed sea. The angel calls them four kings, then speaks of a fourth kingdom (Daniel 7:2-3, 17, 23). The images therefore represent rulers together with the realms they embody.

- The winged lion is commonly read as Babylon. Its humbled, humanized condition recalls Nebuchadnezzar's humiliation and restoration (Daniel 4:28-37; Daniel 7:4).
- The bear raised on one side is commonly read as Medo-Persia. Daniel does not name the three ribs, so identifications of three specific conquests remain historical proposals (Daniel 7:5).
- The four-headed, four-winged leopard is commonly read as Greece and its divisions. Daniel 8 explicitly says that four kingdoms arise after the Greek ruler's great horn is broken (Daniel 7:6; Daniel 8:21-22).
- The terrifying fourth beast has iron teeth, ten horns, and a little horn that uproots three. It devours, crushes, boasts against God, and persecutes the saints (Daniel 7:7-8, 19-25). Rome is the common Christian identification of the fourth kingdom. The final form of its horns and little horn remains disputed.

The point of the chapter does not depend on decoding every horn before it appears. The Ancient of Days sits. The court opens the books. The beast loses its dominion. Judgment is given in favor of the saints (Daniel 7:9-12, 21-22, 26).

### One like a son of man

Daniel sees "One like the Son of Man" coming with the clouds of heaven to the Ancient of Days. He receives dominion, glory, and a kingdom, and peoples of every nation and language serve Him (Daniel 7:13-14). In the immediate scene, the movement is toward the heavenly throne. The vision portrays royal investiture.

Jesus repeatedly takes the title "Son of Man" and applies Daniel's language to Himself. He tells the high priest that they will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds (Matthew 26:63-64). His words join Psalm 110:1, where the king sits at God's right hand, with Daniel 7:13. The ascension begins that public vindication in heaven (Acts 1:9; Acts 2:32-36). His visible return reveals it to the earth (Matthew 24:30; Revelation 1:7).

Daniel also says that the saints receive the kingdom (Daniel 7:18, 22, 27). The Son of Man is not separated from His people. The King receives the kingdom and shares it with those who belong to Him. The New Testament keeps that order: Christ inherits all things, and those united to Him reign with Him (Hebrews 1:2; Romans 8:16-17; Revelation 3:21; Revelation 20:4-6).

### The three-and-a-half period

The little horn oppresses the saints for "a time, and times, and half a time" (Daniel 7:25). The natural sum is one time plus two times plus half a time, or three and a half. Daniel 12 repeats the expression, while Revelation uses forty-two months and 1,260 days for related periods of trampling, witness, preservation, and beastly authority (Daniel 12:7; Revelation 11:2-3; Revelation 12:6, 14; Revelation 13:5).

The parallel supports a shared three-and-a-half-year pattern. It does not prove that every passage begins on the same day or describes the same actor. The context must decide each use.

## The Ram, the Goat, and the Sanctuary: Daniel 8

Daniel 8 removes some of the uncertainty left by the earlier visions. Gabriel names the ram as "the kings of Media and Persia" and the goat as Greece. The goat's great horn is its first king. When it breaks, four kingdoms arise from his nation, though with less power (Daniel 8:20-22).

The common historical reading identifies the first Greek king as Alexander. Daniel names the kingdom and describes a mighty king whose realm is broken toward the four winds, but it does not give his personal name (Daniel 8:21-22; Daniel 11:3-4).

From one of them comes a little horn. It grows toward the south, the east, and the Beautiful Land. It attacks the holy people, opposes the Prince of princes, removes the regular offering, casts truth down, and profanes the sanctuary (Daniel 8:9-13, 23-25).

Daniel 11 describes a contemptible king who profanes the sanctuary, removes the regular offering, and sets up the abomination that causes desolation (Daniel 11:21, 31-35). This close verbal link supports the historical reading that Daniel 8 and the earlier portion of Daniel 11 describe the same pattern of persecution. The common identification is Antiochus IV. The name comes from historical reconstruction, not from Daniel's text.

The pattern reaches beyond that ancient crisis. Gabriel says the vision concerns "the time of the end" and "the appointed time of the end" (Daniel 8:17, 19). Jesus later warns His disciples about Daniel's abomination of desolation as something they must still heed (Matthew 24:15-22). Paul describes a lawless ruler who exalts himself in God's temple and whom the Lord destroys at His coming (2 Thessalonians 2:3-8). Revelation portrays a beast that blasphemes God and makes war on the saints for forty-two months (Revelation 13:5-7). Scripture itself treats the earlier desecration as a pattern that can return in a final form.

### The 2,300 evenings and mornings

Daniel hears that the sanctuary will be restored after 2,300 evenings and mornings (Daniel 8:13-14). Two principal readings exist:

1. **2,300 full days.** Genesis uses the paired words "evening" and "morning" for a complete day (Genesis 1:5, 8, 13). On this reading, Daniel gives 2,300 days.
2. **1,150 days of missed offerings.** Israel offered one lamb in the morning and another at twilight each day (Exodus 29:38-42; Numbers 28:3-8). On this reading, 2,300 evening-and-morning offerings represent 1,150 calendar days.

Daniel does not explicitly choose between those reckonings. It also does not identify this period with the 1,260, 1,290, or 1,335 days. Similar subject matter does not erase different numbers.

## Jeremiah's Seventy Years and Daniel's Prayer: Daniel 9

Daniel 9 begins with Scripture, not arithmetic. Daniel reads Jeremiah's promise that Jerusalem's desolation will last seventy years (Daniel 9:1-2; Jeremiah 25:11-12; Jeremiah 29:10). He responds with confession. He does not accuse God of forgetting the calendar. He confesses Israel's covenant rebellion and appeals to God's mercy (Daniel 9:3-19).

The exile itself had a Sabbath dimension. Moses warned that a desolated land would enjoy the rests Israel had denied it (Leviticus 26:34-35, 43). Chronicles applies that warning to the Babylonian exile and Jeremiah's seventy years (2 Chronicles 36:20-23). Gabriel's answer expands the horizon from seventy years to seventy sevens for Daniel's people and holy city (Daniel 9:20-24). The setting invites a years-based, sabbatical reading: seventy sevens equal 490 years. The passage calls them "sevens" rather than explicitly saying "years," so the unit comes from this covenant context.

### The structure of the seventy sevens

Daniel 9:24-27 divides the period into three parts:

- Seven sevens: 7 x 7 = 49 years.
- Sixty-two sevens: 62 x 7 = 434 years.
- One final seven: 7 years.

The first two segments total sixty-nine sevens, or 483 years. All seventy total 490 years.

The prophecy moves through a restoring word, a rebuilt Jerusalem, an anointed ruler, the cutting off of an anointed one, the destruction of city and sanctuary, covenant action during a final seven, the ending of sacrifice at its midpoint, and determined desolation (Daniel 9:24-27).

The sequence is clear. Several identities and intervals are not. Daniel does not supply a BC date, name the Persian decree, define the calendar length of each year, identify every pronoun, or explicitly state that a gap interrupts the final seven.

## The Six Purposes of the Seventy Sevens

Gabriel gives six purposes before he gives the timetable (Daniel 9:24). The goals control the calculation. A date model that loses the work of God against sin has lost the center of the prophecy.

### 1. To finish transgression

Daniel has confessed a long history of covenant rebellion: Israel rejected God's commands and ignored His prophets (Daniel 9:5-11). "Transgression" therefore concerns more than isolated mistakes. It names revolt against God.

Jesus confronts that history when He warns Jerusalem's leaders that they are filling up the measure of their fathers and will persecute His messengers (Matthew 23:29-36). His death exposes human rebellion at its fullest, yet His prayer for forgiveness also reveals God's answer to it (Luke 23:33-34; Acts 2:22-24, 36-39).

Christ's first coming decisively judges transgression, but the New Testament still anticipates Israel's future turning and the removal of ungodliness (Romans 11:25-27). This creates an accomplished-and-consummated pattern. The cross defeats rebellion at its root; the kingdom brings that victory to its appointed completion.

### 2. To make an end of sins

Christ appeared "once for all" to put away sin by the sacrifice of Himself (Hebrews 9:26). He died to sin once and now lives to God (Romans 6:9-10). Those statements support a first-coming fulfillment.

Sin has not vanished from human experience. Believers still confess sin and await the redemption of their bodies (1 John 1:8-9; Romans 8:22-23). Christ will appear a second time, not to bear sin again, but to save those waiting for Him (Hebrews 9:27-28). Daniel's purpose can therefore include both the decisive end secured by the cross and the final removal displayed in resurrection and judgment.

### 3. To make atonement for iniquity

This purpose points most directly to Messiah's sacrificial work. Isaiah's Servant is pierced for transgressions, bears iniquity, and makes many righteous (Isaiah 53:5-6, 10-12). Jesus says His blood of the covenant is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins (Matthew 26:28). Animal blood could never take away sins; Christ's single offering does what those repeated sacrifices could not and perfects His people (Hebrews 10:4, 10-14, 18).

The destruction of the temple did not create atonement. Christ's death did. The torn veil announces access to God through His body before the sanctuary falls (Matthew 27:50-51; Hebrews 10:19-22).

### 4. To bring in everlasting righteousness

God reveals His righteousness through faith in Jesus Christ while remaining just and the justifier of the one who believes (Romans 3:21-26). Christ becomes righteousness for His people, and in Him they become the righteousness of God (1 Corinthians 1:30; 2 Corinthians 5:21).

Daniel's wording reaches beyond individual justification. "Everlasting righteousness" matches the everlasting kingdom of Daniel 2 and Daniel 7 (Daniel 2:44; Daniel 7:14, 27). The prophets expect a world in which righteousness dwells openly (Isaiah 11:1-9; 2 Peter 3:13). The first coming establishes the righteous basis and forms a righteous people. The return completes the public reign.

### 5. To seal vision and prophecy

"Seal" can carry more than one sense. It can mean authenticate or confirm, as a seal certifies what is true. Jesus fulfills what was written in the Law, the Prophets, and the Psalms concerning Him (Luke 24:25-27, 44-47). His fulfillment confirms the prophetic word.

It can also mean bring the prophetic vision to its appointed completion. Daniel is told to seal the book until the time of the end because parts of the vision remain future (Daniel 12:4, 9). On that reading, this fifth purpose is not exhausted until all that God revealed reaches fulfillment.

Both senses keep the focus on God's faithfulness. The prophecy is neither discarded nor left unfinished.

### 6. To anoint a most holy place

The phrase can be translated "most holy" or "Most Holy." Elsewhere in the Old Testament, the same form usually describes a sacred place, altar, offering, or object rather than a person (Exodus 26:33-34; Exodus 29:37; Exodus 30:29; Leviticus 2:3; Ezekiel 43:12). This supports a sanctuary reading. Daniel has been praying about God's desolate sanctuary, so that context deserves weight (Daniel 9:17).

Other readers connect the phrase to Messiah because Jesus is the Anointed One in the following verses and presents His body as the true temple (Daniel 9:25-26; John 2:19-21). Hebrews locates the greater sanctuary in heaven, entered by Christ with His own blood (Hebrews 9:11-12, 23-24). Ezekiel also looks forward to God's sanctuary among His people forever (Ezekiel 37:26-28).

The options are related but not identical: an anointed restored sanctuary, Messiah as the holy meeting place between God and humanity, the heavenly sanctuary, or the final dwelling of God with His people. Daniel 9:24 does not settle the choice by itself.

## The Restoring Word That Starts the Sixty-Nine Sevens

Daniel dates the count from the issuance of the decree to restore and rebuild Jerusalem (Daniel 9:25). Scripture records several Persian actions connected with restoration.

The ultimate authority belongs to God. Gabriel has already said that a word went out when Daniel began to pray (Daniel 9:23). Isaiah says God confirms His messenger's word, declares that Jerusalem will be inhabited, and appoints Cyrus to rebuild the city and lay the temple's foundation (Isaiah 44:26-28; Isaiah 45:13). Ezra says the Lord stirred Cyrus to issue his proclamation in fulfillment of Jeremiah (Ezra 1:1). The divine word and the royal command can therefore belong to one action: God decrees restoration and a king publishes and executes it.

### One interval or two anointed figures

Daniel 9:25-26 can be read with the seven and sixty-two sevens joined into one sixty-nine-seven interval leading to Messiah. This produces the unified Messianic calculation developed below.

Another reading places a stronger break after the first seven sevens. An anointed prince appears after seven sevens, Jerusalem is rebuilt through the following sixty-two, and an anointed one is then cut off (Daniel 9:25-26). Cyrus is a scriptural candidate for the first figure because God calls him His anointed and commissions him to rebuild Jerusalem (Isaiah 44:28; Isaiah 45:1, 13). Daniel does not name either figure in these verses, so the punctuation and identifications remain interpretive.

### Cyrus: the return and the temple

Cyrus authorizes the exiles to return and rebuild the house of the Lord in Jerusalem (2 Chronicles 36:22-23; Ezra 1:1-4). Isaiah had already named Cyrus as the ruler through whom Jerusalem would be rebuilt and the temple founded (Isaiah 44:26-28; Isaiah 45:13). This decree has powerful prophetic credentials, but Ezra's wording concentrates on the temple.

### Darius: confirmation of the temple decree

Darius searches the archives, confirms Cyrus's order, funds the work, and protects it (Ezra 6:1-12). Ezra summarizes the temple's completion as occurring under the commands of Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes (Ezra 6:14-15). Darius continues the restoration, but his recorded decree does not provide the usual starting point for the sixty-nine sevens.

### Artaxerxes and Ezra: law, government, and restoration

In Artaxerxes' seventh year, Ezra receives a royal commission. The king authorizes return to Jerusalem, supplies the temple, grants broad financial privileges, and commands Ezra to appoint magistrates and judges who will enforce God's law (Ezra 7:7-9, 11-26). This reaches beyond construction into the restored civic and covenant order of Jerusalem.

Ezra later says that the Persian kings gave Judah a "wall" and revived God's house (Ezra 9:9). Earlier opponents had described the returned exiles as rebuilding Jerusalem's walls and foundations, then obtained an order to stop them until a further command should be issued (Ezra 4:12-13, 21). These texts form the scriptural case for treating Artaxerxes' commission to Ezra as the effective word to restore Jerusalem, not merely its temple.

### Artaxerxes and Nehemiah: the walls named directly

In Artaxerxes' twentieth year, Nehemiah asks permission to rebuild the city where his fathers are buried. The king grants the request and issues letters for safe passage and timber for the gates and wall (Nehemiah 2:1-8). Nehemiah then tells Jerusalem's leaders to rebuild the wall, and they begin the work (Nehemiah 2:17-18).

This is the clearest recorded authorization tied directly to rebuilding the city walls. The account calls it a granted request and records royal letters; it does not quote a formal city-building decree in the same form as Ezra 7. That textual difference explains why one model favors Ezra and another favors Nehemiah.

## Two Major Messianic Calculations

Both calculations seek the same sixty-nine sevens. They differ in starting event, kind of year, and Messianic endpoint.

### The Ezra commission model: 458 BC to AD 26

This model dates Artaxerxes' commission associated with Ezra to 458 BC and treats each seven as seven ordinary calendar years.

The arithmetic must account for the missing year zero:

`458 + 26 - 1 = 483 elapsed years`

The subtraction removes the nonexistent year zero between 1 BC and AD 1.

That produces AD 26 as the endpoint of the sixty-nine sevens. The model identifies "Messiah the Prince" with Jesus' public anointing and the opening of His ministry. Jesus is baptized, the Spirit descends on Him, and He announces the kingdom (Luke 3:21-23; Luke 4:16-21; Acts 10:37-38).

The date depends on historical reconstruction. Ezra names Artaxerxes' seventh regnal year but does not call it 458 BC (Ezra 7:7-9). Luke dates John the Baptist's ministry to the fifteenth year of Tiberius, but translating that notice into AD 26 depends on how Tiberius's reign is counted (Luke 3:1-3). Daniel also does not explicitly say that the sixty-nine sevens end at Jesus' baptism rather than another manifestation of His kingship.

#### From AD 26 to a spring AD 30 crucifixion

The model then places roughly three and a half years of ministry between a fall AD 26 baptism and a spring AD 30 Passover. John's Gospel names Passovers during Jesus' ministry and provides the main scriptural framework for a multi-year ministry (John 2:13, 23; John 6:4; John 11:55; John 12:1). John 5:1 mentions another feast without naming it, so the precise length still requires interpretation.

On this reading, Messiah is cut off in the middle of the seventieth seven (Daniel 9:26-27). His death establishes the covenant in His blood, completes the pattern of the sacrificial system, and provides the once-for-all offering that alone removes sin (Matthew 26:28; Hebrews 9:11-15; Hebrews 10:4, 10-18). The remaining half-seven is then reserved for a future period of distress.

This split-half view draws support from the recurring three-and-a-half periods in Daniel and Revelation (Daniel 7:25; Daniel 12:7; Revelation 11:2-3; Revelation 12:6, 14; Revelation 13:5). Its pause, however, is an inference. Daniel 9 does not say that the clock stops after Messiah's death, and the two halves are separated by a long interval only if the reader supplies that structure.

### The Nehemiah commission model: 445 BC to AD 32

The traditional prophetic-year model starts with Nehemiah's commission in Artaxerxes' twentieth year, commonly dated to 445 BC (Nehemiah 2:1-8). It treats each prophetic year as 360 days:

`69 sevens x 7 years x 360 days = 173,880 days`

That span equals about 476 solar years. Counting 476 years from 445 BC, with no year zero, reaches AD 32. The model associates the endpoint with Jesus entering Jerusalem as the publicly presented king (Luke 19:28-44; compare Zechariah 9:9).

Some forms of the calculation place the entry on Nisan 10 and connect it with the day Israel selected the Passover lamb (Exodus 12:3). John places the entry after Jesus came to Bethany six days before Passover, but he does not label the entry Nisan 10 (John 12:1, 12-15). The lamb connection therefore depends on the reconstructed calendar as well as typology.

Scripture sometimes uses 1,260 days, forty-two months, and three and a half times for equivalent periods. Those equations imply thirty-day months and a 360-day schematic year within those apocalyptic passages (Revelation 11:2-3; Revelation 12:6, 14; Revelation 13:5). The model extends that year length to Daniel 9.

Daniel 9 itself does not specify 360-day years. Nehemiah gives the month Nisan but not the day on which the king granted his request (Nehemiah 2:1). Luke does not date the triumphal entry by a Roman calendar. The 173,880-day endpoint therefore depends on reconstructed start and end dates as well as the prophetic-year premise.

### Shared result, separate assumptions

Both models make a serious scriptural case:

- Ezra 7 supplies a formal royal commission with authority to restore worship, law, and government. Its ordinary-year count can reach the opening of Jesus' ministry.
- Nehemiah 2 names the ruined city and wall directly. Its 360-day count can reach Jesus' royal entry.

Neither calculation can be treated as a verse. The Ezra model must defend its historical year, ministry endpoint, and split half-week. The Nehemiah model must defend its exact day, 360-day conversion, and AD 32 Passion chronology. Both agree on the central claim Daniel makes: Messiah appears, Messiah is cut off, Jerusalem and the sanctuary face destruction, and God's purposes do not fail (Daniel 9:24-27).

## The Final Seven and the Identity of "He"

Daniel 9:27 says that "he" confirms or makes a strong covenant with many for one seven. In the middle of the seven, he causes sacrifice and offering to cease. An abomination brings desolation until the decreed end is poured out on the desolator.

The pronoun creates the main interpretive divide. The nearest named figures are the anointed one who is cut off and the coming prince whose people destroy the city and sanctuary (Daniel 9:26). Hebrew grammar alone has not ended the dispute, so the whole biblical pattern must be considered.

### Messianic covenant reading

In this reading, the subject is Messiah. Several scriptural links support it:

- God promises a covenant through His Servant, who bears the sins of many (Isaiah 42:6; Isaiah 49:8; Isaiah 53:11-12).
- Jesus says His blood of the covenant is poured out "for many" for forgiveness (Matthew 26:28; Mark 14:24).
- Christ confirms God's promises and opens mercy to the nations (Romans 15:8-12).
- His once-for-all offering fulfills the sacrificial pattern and alone removes sin; animal blood never could (Hebrews 9:11-15, 26; Hebrews 10:4, 10-18).

Under a continuous first-century form of this reading, the final seven runs through Christ's ministry and the early apostolic witness. Some place its close near the witness and death of Stephen, when the gospel begins a major outward movement (Acts 6:8-15; Acts 7:54-60; Acts 8:1-5). Acts does not call Stephen's death the end of Daniel's seventieth seven, so that endpoint remains a chronology rather than an explicit identification.

Under the split-half form, Christ confirms the covenant and is cut off at the midpoint. The remaining three and a half years await the final crisis. This joins Messiah's first and second comings within one seven. The repeated half-week periods support the shape, but the long pause remains unstated in Daniel 9.

### Future-ruler reading

Many futurist interpreters take "he" as the coming prince of Daniel 9:26. A future ruler makes or enforces a seven-year covenant, breaks it at the midpoint, stops sacrifice, and installs the abomination.

This reading connects Daniel 9 with later passages:

- A ruler in Daniel 11 removes the regular offering and sets up the abomination (Daniel 11:31).
- Jesus places Daniel's abomination within His warning about a coming tribulation (Matthew 24:15-22).
- The lawless one exalts himself in God's temple until Christ destroys him (2 Thessalonians 2:3-8).
- The beast exercises authority for forty-two months and persecutes the saints (Revelation 13:5-7).

The future-ruler reading keeps the entire seventieth seven future after a gap following the sixty-ninth. Daniel 9 does place the cutting off of the anointed one and the destruction of city and sanctuary after the sixty-two sevens and before verse 27's final-seven action. It does not state the length of the interval or use the word "Antichrist."

### Historical desecration and recurring pattern

Another reading hears Daniel 9 against the desecration described in Daniel 8 and Daniel 11. The removal of sacrifice and the abomination first take shape in the persecution associated with the Greek successor kingdom (Daniel 8:9-14; Daniel 11:21, 31-35).

That historical pattern does not exhaust the New Testament's use of Daniel. Jesus treats the abomination as a warning with future force (Matthew 24:15). Paul and Revelation develop the same blasphemous, persecuting pattern toward the return of Christ (2 Thessalonians 2:3-8; Revelation 13:5-8). Daniel's earlier oppressor can therefore serve as a type of a final one without making every detail identical.

## The Final Conflict and the Numbered Days: Daniel 10-12

Daniel's last vision begins with spiritual conflict behind earthly kingdoms (Daniel 10:12-21). Chapter 11 traces wars, alliances, betrayals, persecution, and desecration. Chapter 12 then widens the horizon to unequaled distress, deliverance, resurrection, and everlasting destiny (Daniel 12:1-3).

Jesus uses Daniel 12's language for the great tribulation (Matthew 24:21; compare Daniel 12:1). He also teaches the same two-outcome resurrection: those in the graves rise either to life or to judgment (John 5:28-29; compare Daniel 12:2). Daniel's end point is not merely the survival of a nation. It is resurrection.

### Time, times, and half a time: Daniel 12:7

The heavenly figure swears that the period will last "a time, and times, and half a time." The completion comes when the shattering of the power of the holy people ends (Daniel 12:7). This matches the period in which Daniel 7's little horn persecutes the saints (Daniel 7:25).

Revelation renders the same basic span as 1,260 days, forty-two months, and time-times-half a time (Revelation 11:2-3; Revelation 12:6, 14; Revelation 13:5). These passages justify treating those three expressions as equivalent apocalyptic measures.

### 1,290 days: Daniel 12:11

The 1,290 days are counted from the removal of the regular offering and the setting up of the abomination of desolation (Daniel 12:11). This is thirty days longer than 1,260.

The text names the starting conditions but does not explain the additional thirty days. It does not say they are for cleansing the sanctuary, judging nations, burying armies, or preparing a kingdom. Those proposals may be placed in a model, but none should be quoted as Daniel's explanation.

### 1,335 days: Daniel 12:12

A blessing rests on the one who waits and reaches 1,335 days (Daniel 12:12). If the 1,290 and 1,335 counts share the same starting point, the difference is forty-five days. Daniel does not explicitly repeat the start point for the 1,335, nor does he name an event on its final day.

The safest conclusion is the one the angel gives: the faithful must wait. The timetable ends in blessing, not in permission to invent the missing event.

### Why the periods must remain distinct

| Period | Starting point or setting stated in Scripture | Stated conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| 2,300 evenings-mornings | Sanctuary and regular offering are trampled (Daniel 8:13-14) | Sanctuary restored |
| Time, times, half a time | Saints are oppressed and their power shattered (Daniel 7:25; Daniel 12:7) | Persecuting power reaches its limit |
| 1,260 days | Witnesses prophesy; the woman is preserved (Revelation 11:3; Revelation 12:6) | Context supplies the next event, not the number alone |
| 42 months | Holy city is trampled; beast exercises authority (Revelation 11:2; Revelation 13:5) | Authority remains bounded by God |
| 1,290 days | Offering removed and abomination set up (Daniel 12:11) | No separate endpoint event is named |
| 1,335 days | Waiting through the final crisis (Daniel 12:12) | Blessing for the one who reaches it |

The 1,260-day and forty-two-month expressions clearly correspond to a three-and-a-half-year scheme. The 1,290 and 1,335 are deliberately different. Scripture gives no warrant for forcing them back to 1,260 through undocumented calendar corrections.

## Resurrection, Inheritance, and the End of Daniel's Timeline

Michael arises during a time of distress unlike any before it. Everyone among Daniel's people who is found written in the book is delivered. Many who sleep in the dust awake, some to everlasting life and others to shame and everlasting contempt (Daniel 12:1-2).

The wise shine like the brightness of the sky, and those who turn many to righteousness shine like stars forever (Daniel 12:3). Jesus applies similar language to the righteous in His kingdom (Matthew 13:40-43). Revelation's book of life and final judgment develop the same divide between life and condemnation (Revelation 20:11-15).

Daniel asks for more detail, but the angel does not satisfy every chronological question. The words remain sealed until the time of the end. The wise understand through purification and faithfulness, while the wicked continue in wickedness (Daniel 12:8-10). In context, the increase of knowledge concerns understanding the sealed vision. The verse does not predict modern technology.

The book closes with a personal promise: Daniel will rest, then rise to receive his allotted inheritance at the end of the days (Daniel 12:13). "Rest" means death here, but it is not defeat. Resurrection stands beyond it.

## Sabbath Rest, the Third Day, and Prophetic Pattern

Daniel's seventy sevens arise from a Sabbath-shaped setting: seventy years of exile, the land receiving its missed Sabbaths, and a 490-year answer concerning sin, righteousness, and Jerusalem (Leviticus 26:34-35; 2 Chronicles 36:20-23; Daniel 9:2, 24). Daniel 12 closes with rest followed by resurrection and inheritance (Daniel 12:2, 13).

Other passages use a third-day pattern for restoration. Hosea says that after two days God will revive His people and on the third day raise them up to live before Him (Hosea 6:1-2). Jesus says the Scriptures require the Messiah to suffer and rise on the third day (Luke 24:46; compare 1 Corinthians 15:3-4). Hebrews presents a Sabbath rest still awaiting God's people and roots it in God's completed creation work (Hebrews 4:1-11; Genesis 2:2-3).

These themes belong together in Christ: suffering gives way to resurrection, labor gives way to God's rest, and exile gives way to inheritance. They do not create a conversion rule in which every prophetic day equals one thousand years. Psalm 90:4 and 2 Peter 3:8 compare a thousand years with a day from God's eternal perspective; neither passage supplies a formula for dating Daniel's visions. Peter's conclusion is patience, repentance, holiness, and hope for a world where righteousness dwells (2 Peter 3:8-14).

## A Step-by-Step Integrated Timeline

The following sequence harmonizes Daniel's separate visions with explicit New Testament connections. Several steps synchronize material from different chapters, so their placement involves interpretation. The sequence does not assign an undisclosed calendar date to Christ's return.

1. **Babylon holds Jerusalem in exile.** Daniel serves under Babylon and then Medo-Persia (Daniel 1:1-7; Daniel 5:28-31; Daniel 6:28).
2. **God permits a sequence of earthly kingdoms.** Daniel 2 and Daniel 7 show four kingdoms before the open triumph of God's kingdom (Daniel 2:36-45; Daniel 7:17-27).
3. **A restoring word goes out under Persian rule.** Cyrus, Ezra's Artaxerxes commission, and Nehemiah's authorization each contribute to the restoration record; the choice of starting decree belongs to chronology (Isaiah 44:26-28; Ezra 1:1-4; Ezra 7:11-26; Nehemiah 2:1-8; Daniel 9:25).
4. **Medo-Persia and Greece receive explicit names.** Daniel 8 supplies the identifications and shows a fourfold division after Greece's first king (Daniel 8:20-22).
5. **A desecrating ruler establishes the pattern of the final opponent.** He removes sacrifice, casts down truth, and persecutes the faithful (Daniel 8:9-14, 23-25; Daniel 11:21, 31-35).
6. **Messiah appears and is cut off.** Jesus is anointed by the Spirit, announces the kingdom, gives His life for many, and establishes the new covenant in His blood (Daniel 9:25-27; Luke 3:21-23; Luke 4:18-21; Matthew 20:28; Matthew 26:28).
7. **Christ ascends and the gospel goes to the nations.** The Son of Man receives authority, and His witnesses carry the message to the ends of the earth (Daniel 7:13-14; Matthew 28:18-20; Acts 1:8-9; Acts 2:32-36).
8. **Jerusalem and the sanctuary face destruction.** Daniel places this after Messiah is cut off; Jesus later warns Jerusalem of coming desolation (Daniel 9:26; Luke 19:41-44; Luke 21:20-24).
9. **A final blasphemous opposition persecutes God's people for a bounded time.** Daniel, Jesus, Paul, and Revelation share this pattern, though interpreters differ over how their details align (Daniel 7:21-26; Daniel 12:1, 7; Matthew 24:15-22; 2 Thessalonians 2:3-8; Revelation 13:5-8).
10. **The heavenly court judges and Christ's kingdom is revealed.** The beast loses dominion, the Son of Man reigns, and the saints receive the kingdom (Daniel 7:9-14, 22, 26-27; Revelation 11:15-18).
11. **The dead rise.** Some awake to everlasting life and others to judgment (Daniel 12:2-3; John 5:28-29; Revelation 20:11-15).
12. **The faithful receive their inheritance.** Daniel rests and rises for his portion; the saints share the everlasting kingdom (Daniel 7:18, 27; Daniel 12:13; Romans 8:16-17).

## Reading the Dates Without Claiming What God Has Not Said

Daniel expects readers to study, compare, and understand (Daniel 9:2; Daniel 12:4, 10). He also records limits. Daniel himself does not understand every detail, and the angel answers some questions with a command to go his way (Daniel 12:8-9, 13).

Jesus commands watchfulness, but He does not turn watchfulness into ownership of the Father's schedule (Matthew 24:36, 42-44; Acts 1:6-8). Paul says believers should recognize the character of the times and live awake, sober, and faithful (1 Thessalonians 5:1-11). The purpose of prophecy is not to make speculation sound certain. It is to anchor endurance in the certainty of God's kingdom.

Daniel's final word is therefore stronger than a date. Empires end. The court sits. Messiah reigns. The dead rise. The faithful inherit the kingdom.
